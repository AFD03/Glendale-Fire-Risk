3D Visualization of Wildfire Risk in Glendale, CA

An interactive, building-level view of wildfire vulnerability using terrain, vegetation, and building data.

Wildfires move faster on steep, dry slopes and on south-facing aspects; vegetation supplies the fuel. This project brings those elements together into a data-driven, 3D map of Glendale — letting planners, emergency managers, and residents explore how terrain, vegetation, and buildings combine to create local risk.

Why this matters

Glendale sits at the edge of the Verdugo foothills where topography and vegetation create strong local variations in fire behavior. A spatial, interactive view helps translate risk into actionable insight: where to prioritize defensible space, targeted inspection, or vegetation management.

How the model works (high level)

  • Slope: steeper slopes increase spread — higher slope → higher terrain risk.
  • Aspect: south-facing slopes receive more sun and dry out faster — factored into risk.
  • Vegetation: fuel type and density are mapped from public sources and combined with drought indicators.
  • Buildings: building footprint, elevation, and proximity to high-risk terrain are combined to compute a building-level risk score (1–5).

Key findings (summary)

  • High and extreme risks cluster along the foothill neighborhoods adjacent to steep canyons.
  • South-facing slopes show consistently higher terrain risk in our weighted overlay than north-facing slopes.
  • Comparison to CalFire’s FHSZ shows both agreements and local differences driven by up-to-date vegetation & DEM-derived slope.

Data sources

DEM (USGS), vegetation layers (CalFire), building footprints (LA county ARCGIS), and historical fire perimeters (FRAP).
